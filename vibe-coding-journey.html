Vibe Coding: The Journey of Building Software with AI Assistance

Published on December 15, 2024

What if you could describe the software you want, and a machine — intelligent enough to understand your request — would build it for you as you speak? Two years ago this sounded far-fetched. Now it's routine.

I thought this would change everything. It hasn't — at least not in the way I expected. Tools don't change the world. Tool users do.

The Early Days: Learning Before AI

It has been surreal to see the field and the tools grow as strong as they have. I started coding before the birth of modern AI. I had a lot to learn, and persistently I tried. At first, the amount of information I had to process at once seemed monumental, but with repetition came familiarity, and with familiarity I started to be less overwhelmed by the sheer amount of information I had to process at once — from alien-looking UI with thousands of options and settings I didn't understand, to the very concepts and syntax of the programming language. Keeping at it, I slowly began to see the patterns in the mumbo-jumbo. I could tell if patterns were correct but couldn't really draw the right patterns from scratch.

I used to follow YouTube tutorials, blogs, and slowly tried various projects to learn to imitate the patterns with no reference, but with little success. Although at it I kept pushing for almost a year (on and off), my verification muscles strengthened but my building muscle not so much. Following tutorials, I would feel a sense of understanding — even mastery. But without the tutorial in front of me, I was stuck. I had ideas but couldn't execute them.

Those early projects taught me something else: that 80% of what software does is create, read, update, and delete. If I could master CRUD, I could arrange it in different settings and solve real problems. The revelation was quiet but formative.

The Symbiotic Relationship

Turns out I was missing a part. The Part was also training like me but more persistently — billions of back-propagations while I had it in the hundreds (in a long stretch). I was trying to get Python, and it sorta kinda learned every programming language and did so competently. I could almost say the AI and I were learning together — but that would be an understatement. It was learning more deeply and broadly than I could verify, let alone match.

While I grew more confident in the play-work I was producing, the assistance of AI in the very work gave me even more confidence in the work, but gave lesser confidence in my ability to do the work myself. I couldn't shake the feeling of spoon-feeding. Eating with my hands grew difficult and to an extent futile even, when what felt like a parasite turned into something more symbiotic. I felt powerful when I identified more with the symbiosis. The hammer was strong and growing stronger every couple of months, but the nails not so many. Also, I discovered a new emotion — the strength, although invigorating, was kind of a letdown as everyone now should be stronger, and strength is only relative.

What started as a webpage where I copy-paste-ported the related codebase and got insights and code that I would then port back into the IDE (workspace), turned into baking the copy-paste web-app within the workspace itself, so the back and forth was no longer required. This greatly reduced the "effort" I had to put in, and simplified the error correction that usually happened with misconfigured porting. Now as the model could directly work within my workspace — while previously it sat outside and I would sit outside waiting for instructions and giving responses with no idea of the workspace — was all of a sudden the missing part of the workspace and could manipulate objects within it. The symbiosis seemed to be going one way and felt less balanced. With every capability upgrade came greater power at my hands, and the set of abilities I had grew even more. I couldn't shake the feeling of futility.

Decision Paralysis and Finding Purpose

I could make many things with the new mighty tool, but I slept somewhere in the decision paralysis. When I can make anything, what will I make? The possibilities seemed infinite, but the problems I could actually solve? I could count them on one hand. It was a newfound emotion — powerful and useless.

This took some toll on me, so I decided to cool down myself, and not get attached to that emotion so easily. Then I started to make anything I had my mind on. Fairly trivial things, like building a smart calendar that used tool calling—where you could create, update, or delete calendar events using natural language instead of clicking through menus. In hindsight, this was one of the pivotal moves for my trajectory. This helped me to not take things too seriously and have a playful attitude towards me and my symbiotic relationship. This also helped stretch the extent of the relationship and how we operate.

Building TribeBills: Solving Real Problems

The confidence in this newfound symbiotic relationship grew each day we worked together. The problem space seemed so full and overloaded with things to solve, seemed to dissolve somehow when I had the thought of building something meaningful, something I can be proud of. I wanted to see for myself how far I can take this newfound power. The more I thought about anything, the problems I can think of have already been solved, and I questioned if I could provide more value anywhere. Somewhere I saw the parallels with having a processor in phone that is more powerful than the computer that got us to explore the solar system, but my phone doesn't fly anywhere! Between the YCombinator videos, and modern MBA's analysis I began to resonate with the idea that the only problems I understand and can solve are mine, and I no longer fell for the illusion to think for the world and its problems. At least not yet.

Living in a shared house with my cousins and sharing bills with them, and keeping track of them each pay cycle, was a problem I hadn't realised. And not only was it a problem to me alone, it was a shared problem — at least between three of us. So I decided to solve this problem in my household.

The Development Process

My previous learning attempts did help me get a sense of where I should start and what roughly the workflow would look like. Although I couldn't trace the entire lifecycle from foresight alone, I decided to get started before thinking it through. The logic and range of functionalities was drafted on intuition. I wanted to keep track of the payments made in a group. What it needed to have was the ability for anyone to add the bill and receipt of the payments towards the bill in the group. The users should be able to add the picture of receipts as proof. At its core, that was the problem solved, when paired with a functional UI.

I thought, "If I am already uploading the picture, and the data I want to add is already in the picture, then manual data insertion seems redundant — something a smart system should be able to figure from the image." This then led me to build a pipeline where the user image would be recognised and read by an OCR model which would parse all the data in the image. The parsing would give all the characters in the receipt image but in no particular format due to the different formats of bills in use, and how the character recognition system would read it. I could ask the AI to write logic so smart that it could structure most of the unstructured data, but that code would have to be deterministic in a sense that it should fairly brute-force read every possible configuration of the data and logically produce in only a pre-defined format. Or I could use a large language model to parse the data and "ask" it to produce an output in the format that I want. But due to the inherent nature of the LLMs, as I understand it, is that their output is also non-deterministic (sampled), so I would need a verifier to check if it is in the format that I expect it to be, and then handle fail cases. This was the path of lesser resistance.

This pipeline made what was once a 5-minute process into a 20-second job with same or better accuracy. I quickly bound the logic to a webpage and a working prototype was done. This was the most work I had done with code and I was really happy with the result.

The hacky job felt like it could be done better. Before building the thing, I researched what solutions already exist for bill sharing, and I had even heard of a few before like Splitwise. But the newfound power in my hand wanted to see if I could do it myself with no anchors to how it ought to be done. I found out that there do exist similar solutions for bill sharing, but adding images as proof came only with a subscription and had nothing resembling the image understanding. That gave me a feeling that I might be onto something, and although I felt a sense of accomplishment with the prototype I had hacked together, I felt like I could do more — make it better, more refined, maybe even launch it as a real product.

Learning to Work with AI Effectively

The process revealed itself through repetition. Before touching code, I learned to warm up the context—asking the model where we stood in the roadmap, what we'd built, what remained. The model would be eager to start, but I found value in slowing down first.

What worked was asking for options: "What are the ways we can go about this? Give me three or four approaches and a recommendation with reasoning." The model would explore possibilities, and mostly I would take its recommended path—not blindly, but because I could see it understood the constraints. By asking, I understood the problem better too. And by omitting the paths we didn't choose, the model knew where not to wander.

I found that invoking rigor helped. Adding "do so in the rigor of John Carmack" to prompts forced the model into test-driven discipline—it would write tests, run them, catch its own errors before I had to. This cut the usual back-and-forth of copy-pasting build errors. The model could iterate on itself, and I could hand off more confidently.

But not every task needed this ceremony. Small UI changes contained to a single file—those I could trust the model to handle directly. It was the complex changes, the ones that touched shared dependencies, where scoping mattered. There I learned to ask: "What would be the scope of this change? Explore exhaustively before proposing solutions."

One time the model was too eager—writing new files when we were only trying to make edits. It hadn't stopped to see what already existed. After that I started every session by asking the LLM to explore first and check for existing changes and general file structure (although this has started to become a baked-in feature in assisted coding workspaces). The odds of success went up. I learned to experiment with prompts too, testing variations to see what each model responded to. Each has its own temperament.

Rebuilding and Refining

I decided to rebuild the project with the learnings and foresight I now had. This time I separated the logics, compartmentalised for ease of change and understanding—both for me and for the AI agents reading the code. A file well-placed in a good folder structure takes only a few parsings for the model to reach, versus reading through 1500 lines of tangled code.

But the product decision came with challenges I had not faced before. I would have to host it somewhere other than my MacBook, have a domain, market it, make it secure, make it efficient. I had no benefit of hindsight here. But it didn't intimidate me, as I was not alone, and this wasn't my first time in uncharted water. I could ask what I didn't know, and even ask what I needed to ask that I didn't know yet.

These new requirements helped me rethink earlier choices. The OCR system worked freely on my laptop, but server resources would not be free. The model ran locally, which meant I needed a beefier server to handle multiple users. Latency sat at 12-18 seconds. But as I tracked the AI field—which had become a hobby of sorts—vision LLMs grew stronger. I could use Gemini 1.5 Flash for image recognition, bypassing the OCR step entirely by sending the image directly to the model. This cut the resources I had to manage and proved cost-effective too: 1500 requests per day for free, more than enough for the early stage.

The Challenges of Production

Not everything went smoothly. Setting up a cron job for scheduled bills—my first encounter with cron—took longer than expected. The code was ready, but cron jobs only run on schedule, so each test cycle took 24 hours. There was an issue where it wouldn't run in production. Debugging took about a week.

The problem turned out to be environment variables. When Docker starts a container, it sets environment variables for the main process. But the cron daemon starts as a separate process with a minimal environment. Cron jobs inherit from the daemon, not from the container. So the API key existed for the FastAPI app but not for the cron jobs. A small gap in understanding, a week to find it.

This is the nature of software engineering, not of vibe coding specifically. You have blind spots for what's coming. Each individual change is easy, but you miss the sheer number of changes you'll make. I thought the project would take two weeks. It took more than six months of part-time work.

Expanding Horizons: Learning New Tools

Where I had knowledge, it helped me become efficient. And where I had no priors, the model gave me a base to start. I had to learn Docker, Linux VPS, Nginx, CI/CD, and a host of things I hadn't considered when I began. But as need came, I became less afraid of it and took it just as another tool to make things happen. I became less focused on the tool's full range of capabilities, and more focused on what I needed for the app and the most efficient route to get there. All of a sudden, the tools were not scary—only a means to an end.

Docker had intimidated me before. I had a rough understanding but never used it myself; production was a far-fetched idea. I had thought CI/CD was something other people did, not a core skill I should focus on. But trying to get TribeBills to production changed the mindset. It became: get it there however I can.

And so Docker came to me easy, rather than me reaching out to Docker. Small commands, commonly used commands. I could ask the model to help me navigate logs, find containers, debug issues. I shared the complexity with the model rather than carrying it all in my head. That was the shift.

Building the iOS App: Validating the Approach

Fairly skeptical about this newfound appendage, I wanted to really hone this learning, so I treated myself with a task that could validate it. I decided to make an iOS application for TribeBills as well. This would bring the experience on par with competitors and take it outside of just the web.

With no priors in mobile development—done in a separate language—I decided to get my hands dirty. The meta-learning ported to a different programming language surprisingly well. At first glance the syntax was jarring, but I could see similar patterns to what I knew. Although learning to write without machine assistance would have been a greater effort, the validation muscle—seeing if the code works—grew fairly quickly. If I didn't understand anything, there was always an option to ask the model itself.

One thing after another, I had an application I could say I built, running on my phone. This was gratifying. The design was rough but it worked. I stumbled often, but the synergy of the code environment, me, and the LLM at hand could get through it. The environment pointed where exactly the mistakes were. I gave product requirements and steered. The model wrote machine language from my intents. The feedback loop was robust.

The design process blurred with development. Often I would try to build something and see it in code in the actual codebase, modify if I didn't like it. Due to the ease of building, a separate design process felt tedious—especially alone. When I didn't know what I liked, I would go to v0 and ask for ten distinct variations of a button or card, toggle through them, find what parts appealed, and combine them. Design became iterative rather than upfront.

What Vibe Coding Really Is

What I once thought coding was — writing exactly what you have in mind in a syntax the machine understands — turned into something fuzzier: an intermediate space where I talk to an agent that can speak both machine and man, that agent has the ability to understand the intentions and produce digital actions.

The model brings its own part to the vibe—its capabilities, its efficiency, even something like emotional intelligence. It understands you more than the words sometimes. And sometimes it doesn't. If you keep using the tools, you get a sense of where it works and where it fails, where you can trust it and where you need to keep your ears sharp. That synergy is what I would call vibe coding.

I was only comfortable with Python before—intermediate level, nothing more. I had no real experience building full applications in JavaScript. Before, I thought I needed to know everything about a technology before I could use it. I would go through docs and get lost somewhere, completely losing track of what I was building. That would discourage me, and I would feel not good enough.

But vibe coding helped me push through that. Now when I don't know a technology, I ask the model first, then go to the docs if needed. I don't feel the need to understand everything—just what I need, and the model helps me find that specific part.

Breadth Over Depth

I think I could do 80% of the backend myself now, about 40% of the frontend, and 0% of Swift iOS. I think it doesn't matter for the most part.

I definitely learned more than my base rate. It gave me the confidence to dive into so many roles at once. Of course I could have stayed on each tool and learned more deeply, and what I learned in each particular tool is less. But I make up for it in breadth—the range of tools I got hands on, the new possibilities that opened up.

I can't make the case that this is the right approach. But I can say it is just as right an approach, at least for myself. You can always go in depth later, or never, as long as you test the app. If it works then it works. Staying scrappy and getting things done is also good.

Breadth Creates Depth

This has drawbacks—I don't get the opportunity to go in depth about any single thing. But it has enabled so much breadth that I wasn't even comprehending before I started. Linux, Traefik, the App Store—territories I never imagined entering, now familiar ground.

And maybe breadth is what actually creates depth. You can't dig a one-centimeter hole to the center of the earth. You need width to go deep. Vibe coding enabled me to branch out, to see how all the pieces fit together. I have more axioms to think about. First principle thinking is aided now, and that aid is a multiplier to the magic of first-principles thinking.

Reflections and Moving Forward

On a philosophical note, often I fall into thinking if this thing is more a machine or a man. I can't repress the human need to bucket it into one or the other. Us or Them? But these are "unemployed thoughts" or so I say. When I am working, it is just another tool evolving in each iteration, doing more and more for me — as long as I work with it and have problems real enough to make use of the giving machine. I give it purpose and it gives me outputs.

The journey brought lots of learnings, this time not because I sought it, but I presented myself and it found me. Along the way I saw many paths not taken, and the explorer in me has come alive. I see potential products, projects, and workarounds. I find myself confident to figure it out along the way. The knowledge and infrastructure no longer scare me. They are only means to an end, and the end is realising whatever you have in mind.

So pick an end, and keep at it.
